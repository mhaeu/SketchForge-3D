# Changelog

## Unreleased

- **Grouping with grouped holes no longer freezes the page:** Grouping an imported mesh with a group of holes - three countersunk screws in one group, say - could block the browser for minutes. The check that counts which triangles a cutter catches asks "is this point inside that hole?" once per triangle centroid and once per boundary edge, against every cutter: 30,000 triangles and three holes are 90,000 questions. For a plain shape the answer is cheap, because the point is turned into the shape's own frame and compared against a box or an ellipse. For a **group** or an imported mesh there is no formula, so its bounding box decides - and fetching that box means rebuilding the group's whole mesh from its children, thread geometry included. That happened once per point. It now happens once per cutter: the test carries a tester that remembers each shape's box, and it remembers it on the shape object itself and not on its id, because a padded cutter is a new object with the same id and two different objects must not share a box. Measured with a box as expensive as rebuilding a middling mesh (a 96-side, 64-ring cylinder, 12,288 triangles) and 400 questions: 15 ms against 852 ms, and those 15 ms are almost entirely the one build. The point-in-hole test moved out of the editor into a module of its own on the way, so the geometry it decides - a rotated box, a round footprint, a hole lying on its side - is measured too, which it was not before. From layerling 1.59.0 (#214, reported there by @MiniCoop8).
- **The cut edge of a split body can be rounded:** After cutting a part with Split, the edges of the new cut face took no fillet and no chamfer, whatever the size. The reason was not the cut but the way a mesh reached the CAD kernel: as an STL, one face per triangle, sewn and healed afterwards. The volume came out right and the topology came out as a heap of triangles - measured on a 20 mm box with a 96-edge bore, cut by a tilted plane: 201 faces and 400 edges where the body has 102 faces and 300 edges, the cut face itself in several pieces (two of them bare triangles of 90.03 and 63.87 mm2), and one wall carrying **53** edges instead of 4. On that heap the kernel refuses: of 103 selectable edges, 94 failed even on their own. A mesh now goes in as the flat faces it is made of. Triangles that lie in one plane are grown into one region across their shared edges - compared against the seed plane, not the neighbour's, or the 96 facets of a cylinder would drift into one region - each region gets a least-squares plane, and then **every corner is put exactly on the intersection of its planes**: three planes are a 3x3 system, two are a line, one is a foot point. That is the whole point. The cut leaves its vertices on a tilted plane in float32, so the triangles there are flat only to about a ten-millionth of a millimetre, and the kernel's own `unifySameDomain` compares plane equations against 1e-7 and finds them equal in some pairs and not in others - hence the shards. After the snap (the corners move by 1.3e-7 mm, float32's last digit) each boundary runs as straight pieces with the mesh's intermediate points dropped, and the body comes out with 102 faces, 300 edges and the same volume to a hundredth of a cubic millimetre. The four edges of the cut then take a fillet of 0.3, 0.5 and 1 mm, where over STL none of them took 0.3. The honest limit: on this body 108 of 204 sharp edges take a fillet, and the 96 that do not are the edges between the tilted cut and the bore's facets - the same body built exactly in the kernel (box minus a 96-gon prism, cut by a half space) has the same 102 faces, the same 300 edges and the same 108, so that refusal is the kernel's and not ours. A mesh the pass cannot take cleanly - a scan, a figure, anything with a point sitting in the middle of a neighbour's edge - gives `null` and goes the STL way as before; that costs 8 ms for the split half and 14 ms (2048 triangles) to 105 ms (32768) to give up on a sphere. From layerling 1.66.1 (found there in #248).
- **The rotation circle snaps to the ticks it shows:** The protractor drew a tick every 22.5 degrees but only snapped every 45, so half of its ticks were decoration and 22.5 needed Shift. Every tick snaps now, and Shift adds a half one: 11.25 degrees, with finer, paler ticks drawn while Shift is down, so the circle only ever shows marks that something snaps to. Outside the circle nothing changes - a degree by hand, 45 with Shift - because that is a different gesture: there you drag rather than point. The `R` key still turns by 45. From layerling 1.66.5.
- **A 45 degree chamfer is no overhang:** *Show overhangs* marks faces that lean out further than the set angle, and a face lying exactly on the angle - a 45 degree chamfer at the default of 45 - was marked in patches or not at all, depending on the camera: the view compares `-normal.y > limit`, and at equality float noise decides each pixel for itself. The limit now lies a hair above the set angle. How wide a hair is was measured rather than guessed: the normals of a real 45 degree chamfer from the kernel (a 20 mm box, all four lower edges, 3 mm) stay within 1.2e-8 of sin(45 degrees) - they arrive as float32, and that is its last digit - and the view, which pushes the normal across the triangle and normalises it per pixel, adds about 1e-7. The hair is 0.01 degrees, which is 1.2e-4 at the limit: four orders of magnitude above the noise, and a face that really leans out at 45.02 degrees is still marked. From layerling 1.66.5 (#269), which takes 0.4 degrees there - more than the measurement calls for, and enough to keep quiet about a genuine overhang at 45.3 degrees.
- **Holes have the size you typed:** A hole came out 0.1 mm wider than its setting - a 7.9 mm hole measured 8.0 mm, a 6 mm one 6.1 - because the cutter was padded by 0.05 mm on every side. The padding is there for a reason, but only at the two ends of the hole, where a hole that stops flush with a face would otherwise leave splinters: measured on a 20 mm box with a 7.9 mm hole through it, a flush cut gives 2977 triangles against 1034, the same volume, and the cut itself takes **9.9 seconds** instead of 98 ms, because two faces lying exactly on each other break the intersection into slivers. Around the hole there is no padding any more, so width and depth are as typed: the wall of the cut hole now measures 7.8958 mm across the flats of its 96-gon, where it was 7.9957 before, and the 0.0042 mm under the typed size is the polygon the mesh inscribes in the circle - a hole is never wider than typed, which is the side a press fit has to hold on. Measured at the volume removed: 979.63 mm3 now against 1004.59 before, each exactly the 96-gon prism of its width. A hole that only touches a wall - its edge exactly on that face - no longer takes a slab out of it either, because it no longer reaches 0.05 mm into it. From layerling 1.66.3 (#254), reported there by Mondo-chumstyle.
- **Ring gear:** The counterpart to the gear, and not just a gear turned inside out: what is a tooth on a gear is a gap here, so its teeth are pointed inwards and wide outwards, its tip circle lies **below** the pitch circle and its root circle above. And the centre distance is a difference instead of a sum - `module x (ring teeth - pinion teeth) / 2` - which is what makes a planetary set work out: ring, planets and sun, all of one module. It is set like the gear (module, teeth, pressure angle, backlash) plus the rim behind the tooth root, 1.5 module by default, because a thinner printed ring goes soft at the tooth row; the outside diameter is a slider of its own, since a ring usually has to fit a housing. The promise is measured the same way as the gears', with one trap avoided: the inner outline is a closed curve and "inside" it means **in the hole**, so a pinion point sits in the ring's material exactly when it is outside that curve - read the other way round, the measurement says the opposite. A 30-tooth ring and an 18-tooth pinion roll through a whole ring tooth pitch with no overlap and the flanks within 0.06 mm (the resolution of the two outlines, not a gap), and that holds for a 40-tooth ring with a 28-tooth pinion too. Turned the other way the pinion is immediately buried in the ring - internal gearing runs both parts the same way - and one millimetre further out there is no position without overlap. Two limits come with a ring, and the pressure-angle row names the span of pinions that fit between them: the pinion needs enough teeth not to be undercut (`2 / sin^2(pressure angle)`, the same number as the rack's), and the tooth numbers have to differ by at least 10, or the tooth tips butt into each other - measured on a 40-tooth ring, a difference of 10 is free, 8 digs in 0.013 mm and 6 digs in 0.219 mm; on a 60-tooth ring 8 is still free and 6 digs in 0.156 mm. A ring gear is an exact body too - 96 rim faces, 900 faces of the tooth row and two caps, the same volume as the drawn mesh - and the first one whose hole is a drawn outline rather than a circle, so its tooth root can be filleted (which is where a printed tooth breaks). The mesh is built by hand rather than with three.js' extrusion, because that triangulates a hole by ear clipping, which is quadratic in the number of points: a ring took **four seconds** and now takes 12 ms. Both caps are stitched from the two rims by angle, which works because a ring gear tooth does not overhang. From layerling 1.58.1.
- **Rack:** A new shape next to the gear, for turning a rotation into a straight line - a rack-and-pinion drive, a drawer, a linear axis. A rack is the gear with an infinitely large pitch circle, so its flanks are straight lines at exactly the pressure angle, and it is set like a gear: module and number of teeth, which give the length, plus pressure angle and backlash. The length is **exactly** a whole number of pitches (12 x 2 x pi = 75.398 mm by default), because the first tooth stands half a pitch from the end - two racks in a row therefore make one continuous row of teeth with nothing to work out. The depth takes the tooth (2.25 module) plus a back of one module, and nothing less. The promise is measured, not claimed, and it is a stronger one than the gears': a pinion of the same module is rolled along a whole tooth pitch in 120 steps - turned and moved by radius x angle, as rolling without slipping does - and at **every** position of that path there is no overlap and the flanks stay within 0.0097 mm (18 teeth) and 0.0101 mm (24), which is the outline's own resolution and not a gap. A drive that is only free in one position is no drive. One millimetre deeper there is no position without overlap, and a pinion with ten percent more module never fits. One thing a rack brings that two gears do not: below `2 / sin^2(pressure angle)` teeth - 17.1 at 20 degrees, 11.2 at 25 - the rack's tooth tip reaches under the pinion's base circle, where a truly generated gear is undercut and ours is not. Measured at 20 degrees: it digs in 0.006 mm at 14 teeth, 0.021 at 12 and 0.099 at 8. Up to about two hundredths that sits inside the backlash and under what a printer resolves; a tenth does not. So the pressure-angle row says from how many teeth a pinion runs clean, and a wider angle lowers it: at 25 degrees a 12-tooth pinion is already clean. A rack is an exact body too - at twelve teeth 54 planar faces, the same volume as the drawn mesh and as the hand calculation (back plus twelve trapezoids, times the height) - so its tooth root can be filleted, which is where a printed tooth breaks. From layerling 1.58.1.
- **Pick the selection up and set it down on a face:** `C` - or the new button next to Align faces - takes whatever is selected into the hand, and the next click on a face says where it goes. The selection turns so that what was its underside lies on that face, a sloped one too, and lands with its middle over the point clicked, not over the middle of the face. On a face parallel to the workplane it only moves, it does not turn. Several bodies go at once and keep their position to each other, because one turn and one shift hold for all of them and the **lowest** point of the whole selection is the one that touches down - setting each body down for itself is what would pull an assembly apart. Clicking a face of the selection itself says so instead of moving anything: the target would travel along with it. The difference to Align faces is the single click - there you point at two faces and the part keeps its place sideways; here the underside is given and the selection travels. A box set down on a 30-degree slope rests on all four corners of its underside to within 0.016 mm, which is not slack but the stored accuracy: a body carries its three angles to a tenth of a degree, and on this box's 28 mm diagonal three angles rounded that way are exactly those 0.016 mm - the same for Lay flat and Align faces, and far under a layer height. Clicking the empty plate does nothing yet; for that there is still "Drop onto the workplane". From layerling 1.55.0 (#195), the other half of "Cruise", whose first half here is the drop of a new shape.
- **A new shape lands on the face you drop it on:** Dragging a shape onto a body now sets it down on that body's face - a sloped one too - lined up with it, instead of dropping it onto the workplane underneath and leaving it to be pushed up by hand. It lands where the pointer is, not at the middle of the face, and its underside sits on the face: the centre is lifted half its height along the face's normal, not straight up, so it does not dig a corner into a slope. On a face parallel to the workplane - the top of a box, the common case - the placement is exactly what it was before. Over the empty plate nothing changes. From layerling (#195), where the shape also follows the pointer and C picks the selection up again; here it is the drop that lands.
- **Round knurling:** The knurl has a third pattern, Round: grooves and ridges along the axis as a smooth wave of arcs, the same shape as the round gear teeth, with the number of grooves, the depth and the end chamfer free - a pleasant grip for a knob. Round grooves stay shallower than 0.45 of the pitch round the grip, or they would bulge at their foot where the groove gets wider than its mouth; a greater depth gives way, and switching the pattern or the groove count moves the depth with it. A round knurl is an exact body too, chamfered ends included.
- **Round gear teeth:** A third tooth shape, Round: each tooth one arc over the tip and each gap one arc over the root, each running smoothly into the next, like Tinkercad's Useful gear. Small printed gears with them are more forgiving than involute ones, and on a knob they make a smooth grip. They are set by teeth, module and backlash (no pressure angle) and they mesh by module like involute teeth - measured the same way, no overlap at module x (teeth1 + teeth2) / 2 and binding one millimetre closer. They stand lower, tip 0.6 module above the pitch circle and root 0.85 below, so the outside diameter is module x (teeth + 1.2); switching the tooth shape keeps the module and moves the size with it. A round spur gear is an exact body too. That each arc touches the next smoothly is pinned as a number, not a word: the distance between two arc centres is the sum of their radii, and a kink in the tooth root is where a printed tooth breaks.
- **Calculate in number fields:** Every measurement field now takes a calculation, not just a finished number: `+`, `-`, `*` (or `x`), `/` and brackets, point before dash, so "40/2", "120-2*4" and "(40+2)/2" all work, and a decimal comma counts inside one. That is how one measures on a part - half the width, three holes along a length, two wall thicknesses taken off. Before, "40/2" was not a number and the field kept its old value. The calculation is parsed by hand, never evaluated as code. From layerling, asked about by @prmod3d (#180).
- **Gears with involute teeth that mesh:** New gears have involute teeth, set like a gear generator: number of teeth and module, which give the size (outside diameter module x (teeth + 2)), plus pressure angle (20 degrees by default) and backlash for printing (0.2 mm). Straight teeth are now called "Simple" and stay on older designs until the tooth shape is switched. The promise is measured, not claimed: two outlines of the same module, turned against each other in fine steps at the centre distance module x (teeth1 + teeth2) / 2, never overlap and their flanks lie on each other to within 0.015 mm - while one millimetre closer there is no angle without overlap. Checked from 6 to 30 teeth. An involute spur gear is also an exact body, bore included, so its tooth edges can be rounded - a root fillet is what keeps a printed tooth from breaking. One difference to the simple gear: its outline is no longer stretched to fill the bounding box, because that stretch would distort the module by up to 3.4 percent, so an involute gear is a few percent narrower than its nominal diameter - the outside diameter is the tip circle, and a tooth need not point along an axis.
- **SVG import keeps holes again:** A hole inside an outline - a round cut-out drawn in Inkscape, for one - came in filled, as a body of its own. Inkscape closes an outline of arcs about 0.0000002 mm short of its start, and the cleanup kept that sliver because its threshold was fixed at 1e-20; a segment of no length then counted every point as lying on it, so the hole "crossed" the outline. The threshold now scales with the drawing, as the point-in-ring test already did, and a zero-length segment holds only its own point. On the reporter's path the imported area was 4222 mm2 - more than the full 2827 mm2 disc, because the hole was counted on top of it - and is now 1709. From layerling, reported by @AkiraNorthstar (#197), whose path is the regression test.
- **Loose holes are cut on export:** A hole left ungrouped was dropped from an STL, OBJ or SVG export, though the STEP export already cut it - so one design gave a hole or no hole depending on the format, and the filament estimate counted without it. Now every visible hole that goes along is cut out of the bodies it reaches, as grouping would cut it; with a selection, only the selected holes count. Which holes reach a body is decided by their boxes, so a hole at the other end of the plate costs no boolean. If a cut fails, the status line says so and the file holds the bodies uncut rather than silently losing the hole.
- **A twist is drawn as a twist:** A box or a prism has nothing between its bottom and its top, so a twist only turned its lid: the side edges ran straight from one end to the other instead of following the turn, and the body was pinched in the middle - 4.14 mm on a 20 x 20 x 40 box twisted by 90 degrees, where the corner belongs at a radius of 14.14 mm and the straight edge passes at 10.00. The sides also folded along their diagonals, because a quad's two triangles no longer lie in one plane once it is twisted. The mesh is now cut into bands of 7.5 degrees of twist before it is deformed, which leaves less than 0.03 mm of that error, in the view and in the printed file alike. A cylinder was never affected (turning a circle about its own axis changes nothing), and taper, lowered sides and the top offset need no bands - they keep straight lines straight.
- **The height is called Z, as in the slicers:** The position card called the height Y and the depth Z, while the split tool already called the height Z - so our own two tools disagreed, and anyone holding our numbers next to Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer, Tinkercad or a CAD program had two letters swapped. Y now runs towards the back and Z upwards, in the position card and in the pattern panel; the rows are ordered X, Y, Z. Designs do not move and nothing is stored differently - only the labels change. The mapping lives in one place now (`axisLabels.ts`), which the split tool reads too.
- **A straight knurl is an exact body:** Its grooves can now be rounded and chamfered, and it can be hollowed. The outline the kernel pulls up is the same ring of ridges and groove bottoms the mesh is drawn from, so the exact body is the drawn body - measured against the mesh's own volume at 30 and at 78 grooves. A 30-groove grip takes 36 ms to build and has 62 faces and 236 triangles; a 0.15 mm fillet on a groove edge takes 54 ms. The chamfer at the ends is cut as the mesh draws it, by meeting a 45-degree barrel, not by chamfering each rim edge - that would follow the groove flanks as well and leave a different body (4404 against 4418 mm3), and it is slower (8 s against 2.2 s at 78 grooves). With a chamfer the body takes about a second to build, which happens once when an edge tool starts. Crossed knurling stays a mesh: its grooves run on helices, and what two counter-turned rows have in common is not an extruded outline.
- **Align faces:** A new tool next to Lay flat brings a face of the selected part against a face of another part. Click the tool, the part's face, then the other face. "Face to face" puts them back to back, "Flush" lays them in one plane, and a gap leaves room between them (a negative one presses them together). The part turns the shortest way about the clicked point until the faces are parallel - not at all if they already are - and then slides only along the target face's normal, so it keeps its place sideways. A 30-degree-turned, 20-degree-tilted box lands with four corners exactly on the target plane and no point past it. From layerling, asked for by @RobbieKnobbie (#163).
- **Knurling:** A new shape for grips - a round body with grooves all around, for knobs, thumb wheels and tool handles. "Straight" runs the grooves along the axis, "Crossed" lays two slanted rows over each other into small diamonds. Diameter, height, number and depth of the grooves, the chamfer on both ends and, for crossed knurling, the grooves' angle can be set. No groove gets finer than 0.8 mm around the grip - finer than that no FDM printer shows - so the number of grooves is capped by the diameter: 78 on 20 mm, 23 on 6 mm. It sits right after the gear in the shape library. From layerling, which builds straight knurling as an exact CAD body through a profile extrusion we do not have; here it is a mesh, like every shape but the primitives.
- **A revolved sketch is an exact body:** Revolve now builds the body with OpenCascade instead of stacking slices into a mesh. So Hollow, Fillet and Chamfer work on it - hollowing a revolved cup failed before - and it has a few hundred triangles instead of tens of thousands. An arc in the outline stays an arc. Start angle and sweep work as before. A profile that crosses the axis, and the filled variant used for cutting, stay meshes; a body revolved by an older version stays a mesh too, and the edge tools now say so and name the way out (open Edit sketch and finish again). From layerling, reported by @ucito (#167).
- **Hollow can leave any side open:** Instead of top, bottom, both or none, there is a switch for each of the six sides, in any combination: front alone makes a slot for a drawer, left and right a tunnel. Two older faults came out while measuring this. With "bottom" open, hollowing used to fail with "no face found", and "top and bottom" opened only the top: the kernel already returns outward normals, and we turned the ones at the low end of each axis back inwards. And with no side open the body was not hollowed at all but shrunk by the wall thickness - a closed hollow body is now built as the outer body minus the inner one. The wall limit follows the openings: a measure only counts when both of its sides stay closed. From layerling, asked for by @RobbieKnobbie (#159).
- **Patterns can climb and spiral:** A row steps along X, Y and Z at once - a diagonal row, a staircase - instead of along one axis. A circle has a rise per copy (a screw) and a change of radius per copy (a flat spiral); both together give a conical spiral. A spiral running inward stops at the centre instead of throwing its copies through to the other side. From layerling, asked for by @mobiusfix (#145).
- **Small fillets no longer leave a sawtooth:** A fillet of about 0.2 to 0.9 mm round a large circular edge came out with visible zigzags along the seam, in the editor and in the slicer. The mesh strayed 0.1236 mm from the true surface at standard quality - five times the 0.025 mm the quality promises, because one triangle spanned most of the quarter arc. The mesh is now measured against its own faces and, where it strays, rebuilt with a tighter angle (0.1, then 0.05, under 400,000 triangles): 0.0084 mm at standard, 0.0021 mm at fine. Draft quality is left alone, because it keeps its own coarser promise. From layerling (1.45.0).

- **Split:** A new tool in the Modify group cuts the selection in two with a plane. It starts flat - the cut that brings a part too tall for the printer onto the plate - and can be set to any of the three axes, turned about the other two and moved along its normal; a translucent plane shows where it stands. "Pick a face" lays it on the next face you click, tilt included. Every selected body the plane crosses becomes two closed bodies, a hole becomes two holes, and a hollowed body keeps its cavity. The halves are meshes, so their shape settings are gone. From layerling, which took it from a SketchForge fork by WC3D.
- A group resized after grouping is exported, aligned and snapped at the size it shows. Its mesh put the parts together at the size they were grouped at, so STL, OBJ and 3MF got the old size and the filament estimate reported the wrong volume. From layerling, contributed by @gogades (#127).
- "Lay flat on face" lights up the flat face under the pointer before the click, so you can see which side goes down and turn the view to look at the others. On a mesh denser than 20,000 triangles only the facet under the pointer lights up: searching the whole face costs 22 ms at 24,000 triangles, and the click still searches it. From layerling, asked for by @Jeff-Haas (#130).
- A fingertip reaches 16 pixels beside the edge of a body instead of 6, because it covers more than a mouse pointer. The mouse is unchanged. From layerling, contributed by @rmpel (#123).
- Snap grids of your own: in the workspace settings, add a measure with a name and a size in millimetres - the 2.54 mm of perfboard, say - and the snap menu offers it whole, halved and quartered. A step keeps its millimetres, so a design still snaps right after its measure has been removed from the settings. From layerling, contributed by @rmpel (#118).

## 1.1.0

A minor release rather than a patch: everything below has been sitting
unreleased since 1.0.9, and it adds tools rather than only mending them.

New tools

- Hollow turns a solid into a shell with a chosen wall thickness, with any set of sides left open, built on the CAD kernel's `shell`/`offset` (occt-wasm raised to ^5.4.0 for the join type they need). The wall keeps its thickness when the body is resized afterwards instead of growing with it.
- Section view cuts the display along a plane so the inside is visible, without changing the bodies. What the plane hides cannot be clicked, measured, snapped to or pinned a note on; the cut face itself stays reachable.
- Point to point and point to workplane move the first-picked body so the two marked points coincide, or so the marked point lands on the active workplane.
- A target menu decides what a click grabs - free on a surface, free or centred on an edge, the centre of a face, or a corner - shared by point snapping, the rotation pivot and lay flat.
- Lay flat rotates a body so the clicked face rests on the workplane.
- The rotation pivot can be set by clicking a face; the body then turns around that point.
- Pattern repeats the selection in a row or around a circle.
- Cutting tools: trim flush against another body, trim at the workplane, and clear out the interior of a tube or any self-drawn hollow body.
- Three bore shapes join the palette as cutters: counterbore, countersink and teardrop hole.
- An object list shows every body, with range selection by Shift.
- A group can be opened to change its parts without taking it apart.
- Notes sit on the workplane or on a body and travel with the project; they stay out of every export.
- Region resize works on a box inside the object: only what the box holds is stretched, the rest keeps its shape.
- Loft morphs between two cross-sections.
- New shapes: rounded box, honeycomb, ellipse, polygon, thread, spring, ruler, loft, oval, semicircle, pie slice and bolt circle, plus threaded heads (lens head, set screw, Torx).
- Sketch mode gained real circles and arcs, rounded corners, alignment guides, a free rotation handle, a third mode where the shape follows a path, SVG import, and rotate/mirror/scale.
- Extrusion with twist and an offset top face; taper and height per side.

Print preparation

- Overhangs are hatched red and white in the view, from a chosen angle (30 to 70 degrees, 45 by default).
- A warning names the bodies that stand taller than the printer can build.
- The export window estimates volume, weight and filament length for PLA, PETG, ABS, ASA, TPU or PA - worked out as solid, so it is an upper bound.
- 190 printers can be chosen by name; each sets plate width, depth and build height together. The numbers come from OrcaSlicer's profiles.
- The edge tool works on the exact body a STEP import brought with it instead of its triangles, so a round rim is one circle rather than dozens of facet edges.
- 3MF files can be read.
- STEP export carries a body whose edges were filleted or chamfered, rounding included.

Interface

- The toolbar measures itself and steps its icon size down, or wraps, instead of running off the edge - no more zooming the browser to 67 per cent to reach the last group.
- Point tools show a preview of what a click would grab before the click.
- A click on a corner handle types width and length together; Tab moves between them, Enter applies both in one step.
- Shift while dragging holds the body to one axis of the workplane; Alt while dragging leaves a copy behind.
- A box selection with Shift turns each body in it around, as a Shift click does for one.
- The snap grid stays reachable when the body settings are collapsed.
- The interface speaks German as well as English, down to the exercises, messages and shape names.
- Transparency per object, with a slider under the solid/hole choice.
- The arrow keys move a body the way the camera sees it.
- Axis chaining as an L/W/H switch in the toolbar.
- Frame the selection (Shift+F) and an orthographic view button in the camera bar.
- A saved project can be inserted into the open one, and every project can be backed up at once.

Fixes

- A saved project no longer opens into an empty workspace: the reference point was written into the file as a body kind the reader refused, and the failed read then replaced the stored project with nothing. The read now drops what it cannot use, keeps the file, and a failed read never overwrites anything.
- "This page couldn't load" is gone: the app has its own error page, which names the error and offers the way back instead of clearing the project.
- Clicking the gap between two parts of one object no longer selects the object, and neither does dragging a selection box through it. A press that just misses still means the body beside it.
- The reference point no longer ends up inside a group, no longer takes selection handles, and stays out of copies, exports and frame selection.
- Hollowing out an interior now also recognises a rotated tube, a baked body that was resized afterwards, and a self-drawn hollow body.
- Trimming flush now cuts against the body that was clicked last, not the one that was built last.
- The region box no longer slides through the body, keeps its taper, survives a rotation into the workplane, and stops exactly at its own faces.
- A rotated body remembers what it is and how it stands, so its rotation angle and its taper fields stay right.
- Undo brings the workplane back with it.
- Stored CAD geometry is no longer rebuilt as B-splines by a plain move - a fillet's analytic faces survive the next edit.
- Mesh density no longer falls back after later fillets, and fine edges stay clickable in the edge tool.
- Export merges overlapping bodies, so a file no longer holds two shells inside one another.
